Unusual options activity: how to find it and what it means

Unusual options activity is option trading that stands out from a contract's normal behaviour: far more volume than usual, more volume than open interest, or large aggressive orders in contracts that rarely trade. Traders watch it because informed money often moves through options first.

The classic signals

1. Volume far above open interest

Open interest is the number of contracts still open from previous days. When today's volume is several times the open interest, new positions are being opened. A volume/OI ratio above 1 is the single most common unusual activity filter.

2. Volume far above average

A contract that normally trades 200 lots and suddenly trades 8,000 is unusual even if open interest is large. Compare today's volume with the contract's recent average.

3. Aggressive side

Trades at or above the ask (or at or below the bid) show urgency. A cluster of ask-side call sweeps is far more meaningful than the same volume printed mid-market.

4. Short-dated, out-of-the-money contracts

Buying cheap, out-of-the-money options that expire within days or weeks is a leveraged bet on a quick move. It costs little if wrong and pays many times over if right, which is why it shows up before news.

5. Repeat orders

The same contract bought again and again through the day, often in similar sizes, suggests one trader building a position.

Why it is not a guarantee

Most unusual activity does not lead to a big move. Large orders may hedge a stock or futures position, be one leg of a spread, or close an older position. Earnings and other scheduled events naturally inflate volume. Treat unusual activity as a reason to look closer, not as a trade signal.

Questions

What is a good volume to open interest ratio?

A ratio above 1 means today's volume exceeds all open contracts from previous days, which suggests new positions. Many traders look for 2–3× or more together with large premium and ask-side trades.

Does unusual options activity predict stock moves?

Sometimes, but not reliably. Many unusual trades are hedges or spreads. It is best used to find stocks worth a closer look.
