Options flow: how to read sweeps, blocks and big trades

Options flow is the stream of option trades as they print on the exchanges. Reading it well tells you where large traders are putting money: which ticker, calls or puts, which strike and expiry, and how urgently they wanted the trade filled.

What is options flow?

Every option trade in the US prints to the consolidated tape (OPRA) with a price, a size, an exchange and a condition code. Options flow tools collect those prints, add context such as the bid and ask at the moment of the trade, the open interest of the contract and the stock price, and show the biggest and most aggressive ones first.

Most of the tape is noise: market makers hedging, small retail orders and spreads. The trades worth reading are the large, aggressive, single-direction orders, because someone paid real premium and accepted a worse price to get filled now.

The columns that matter

ColumnWhat it tells you
PremiumPrice × size × 100. The money at risk. $1M+ in a single print is significant for most stocks.
SideWhere the trade printed against the quote. At or above the ask means the buyer was aggressive; at or below the bid means the seller was.
TypeSweep: one order split across several exchanges to fill fast. Block: one large negotiated print. Split: one order filled in pieces on the same exchange.
Size vs OIWhen the size is larger than open interest, the trade is very likely opening a new position rather than closing one.
Expiry and strikeShort-dated, out-of-the-money contracts are leveraged bets on a move soon; far-dated, in-the-money ones are often stock replacement or hedges.

Sweeps vs blocks

A sweep takes liquidity from several exchanges at once, paying up at each one. It signals urgency: the trader cared more about getting filled now than about price. Bullish call sweeps at the ask are the classic flow signal.

A block is a single large trade, often negotiated off-screen and printed in one piece. Blocks can be directional, but they are also used for hedges and for closing or rolling positions, so the side (bid or ask) and the open interest matter more.

Bullish or bearish?

Calls bought at the ask and puts sold at the bid lean bullish; puts bought at the ask and calls sold at the bid lean bearish. Trades in the middle of the spread are ambiguous. A flow sentiment gauge simply adds up the premium on each side.

Keep in mind that a single print never tells you the trader's full position. A large put purchase may hedge a stock position; a call sale may be part of a covered call. Look for repetition: the same contract bought again and again across the day is stronger than one big trade.

Questions

Is options flow a reliable signal?

Options flow shows what large traders are doing, not why. Many big trades are hedges or parts of spreads. It is most useful as context, combined with price action, open interest changes and repeated orders in the same contract.

What does buying at the ask mean?

The trade printed at or above the ask price, so the buyer paid the full offer to get filled immediately. It suggests urgency and a buyer-initiated trade.

Is options flow real time?

Exchange prints are published in real time to licensed feeds. On the data feed Options Screener currently uses, option trades can arrive about 15 minutes late; the app labels this on screen.
