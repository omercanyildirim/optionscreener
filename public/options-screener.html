How to use an options screener

An options screener filters thousands of option contracts down to the few that match what you are looking for: the right expiry, the right delta, enough liquidity and the volatility you want. Here is how to set one up.

Why screen options?

A single stock like SPY or NVDA can have thousands of listed contracts across dozens of expiries. Scrolling chains one expiry at a time misses most of them. A screener applies the same rules to every contract at once and ranks what is left.

The filters that matter

FilterUse it toTypical setting
Days to expiry (DTE)Choose the time horizon0–7 for short-term trades, 30–60 for swing trades, 180+ for LEAPS
DeltaChoose how far in or out of the money0.30–0.50 for directional calls; 0.15–0.30 for premium selling
VolumeMake sure the contract trades today100+ contracts
Open interestMake sure there is depth500+ contracts
Bid-ask spread %Avoid contracts that are expensive to tradeBelow 5–10% of the mid price
Implied volatilityFind cheap or rich optionsCompare with the stock's usual IV
Unusual volumeFind new positioningVolume above open interest

Example screens

Liquid swing-trade calls

DTE 30–60, delta 0.40–0.60, volume above 500, open interest above 1,000, spread below 5%. Sort by volume.

Short-term unusual activity

DTE 0–14, unusual volume on, volume above 1,000. Sort by volume and compare with open interest.

Premium selling candidates

DTE 20–45, delta 0.15–0.30 (puts: −0.30 to −0.15), IV in the upper part of its range, spread below 5%.

Liquidity first

Whatever the strategy, start with liquidity. A contract with a wide spread or no open interest can be costly to enter and hard to exit. Volume, open interest and spread filters remove most of the contracts you should not trade anyway.

What is a good bid-ask spread for options?

For liquid stocks and ETFs, spreads of a few cents or under 5% of the option's mid price are common. Wider spreads make the option more expensive to trade.
