Implied volatility explained

Implied volatility (IV) is the volatility that, put into an option pricing model, gives the option’s current market price. It is the market’s price for uncertainty: higher IV means more expensive options for the same strike and expiry.

Reading the number

IV is quoted as an annual percentage. A rough rule converts it to an expected one-standard-deviation move: divide IV by the square root of 252 for one trading day, or by about 3.5 for one month. A stock with 40% IV is priced for daily moves of roughly 2.5%.

Why IV changes

  • Events. IV usually rises before earnings and other scheduled news, then drops once the news is out (often called IV crush).
  • Market stress. When markets fall quickly, demand for puts pushes IV up across the board.
  • Supply and demand. Heavy buying of a particular strike can lift its IV relative to its neighbours.

The IV smile and skew

Plotting IV across strikes for one expiry gives a curve. For most stocks and indexes, out-of-the-money puts carry higher IV than out-of-the-money calls (put skew), because of steady demand for downside protection. A symmetric curve with higher IV at both ends is called a smile.

Questions

Is high implied volatility good or bad?

Neither. High IV means options are more expensive, which matters differently to buyers and sellers.

What is IV crush?

The drop in implied volatility after an expected event such as earnings, once the uncertainty is resolved.
